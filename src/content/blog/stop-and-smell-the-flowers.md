There are days like today when the world feels as though it's moving too quickly to be experienced by the people living in it. Not just lived through, but witnessed.

Every morning brings another flood of headlines, another impossible breakthrough, another reminder that the world we woke up in yesterday no longer exists. Things that would have felt miraculous a year ago barely hold our attention for a day. Before we've had time to make sense of one change, another is already waiting behind it.

We're told this is progress. That this is something to celebrate. That faster is always better. But by the end of the day, when the noise finally gives way to silence, I find myself returning to the same question.

Not what's next?

Not what are we building?

Just—

What is it all for?

Did you know that monarch butterfly populations have declined by more than seventy-five percent since the 1970s? We paved over meadows. We drained wetlands. We replaced native prairies with endless fields of monoculture. We sprayed herbicides that wiped out milkweed, the only plant monarch caterpillars can survive on. We label our native plants that support our native wildlife "weeds" and replace them with expensive Asian exotics. We spend an incredible amount of time making sure our lawns are perfectly green all summer. We spend water, chemicals, and energy maintaining landscapes that are essentially ecological deserts, while the life that once existed there slowly disappears.

But why does this matter? If Star Wars taught us anything, it's that humans don't need butterflies. We don't need forests. We don't need this planet forever. Our technology will save us. Eventually, we'll leave Earth behind and build cities on distant planets like Tatooine.

Whether technology will save us isn't the question I've been asking myself lately. The question is: what exactly are we trying to save ourselves for? A world where we can create anything but have forgotten how to care for anything feels incomplete.

I recently read Robin Wall Kimmerer's Braiding Sweetgrass, and one quote in particular has stayed with me since: "The land knows you, even when you are lost." We often talk about what the Earth gives us—the food, the resources, the materials, the things we extract and transform into the foundations of modern life. We rarely stop to ask what we owe in return. Instead, we find ourselves spending billions of dollars and enormous amounts of energy burning through AI tokens to generate another app, another feature, another piece of software that most people will forget existed within a few years.

And I'm not saying those things are meaningless. Technology is one of the most incredible expressions of human creativity. But there is something strange about living in a time where we can create artificial "intelligence" while becoming increasingly disconnected from the actual intelligence that has existed around us for millions of years. The intelligence of forests that communicate and cooperate. The intelligence of ecosystems that sustain countless forms of life. The intelligence of a monarch butterfly that can find its way across an entire continent without a single line of code.

In our quest to extract as many resources as we can—to build faster, consume faster, and optimize everything—we have lost the ability to notice. To notice when a species disappears. To notice when a river becomes unhealthy. To notice when a place that once felt alive becomes quieter year after year.

Kimmerer also wrote in her book that "paying attention is a form of reciprocity with the living world." In other words, to notice something is to acknowledge that it matters.

When you learn the name of a tree outside your window, when you recognize the birds that return each season, when you stop and smell the flowers around you, you are no longer just looking at the world, but entering into a relationship with it.

That is what's missing from our lives. Not another LLM. Not another SaaS app. Not another tool promising to make us slightly more productive so we can spend our saved time chasing even more things. What we are missing is a relationship with the world around us.

You don't love a person because of their utility. You don't value a friendship because of the things you can extract from it. You don't care for a child because of what they might produce someday. Maybe the first step toward healing isn't some grand technological solution or a massive global initiative. Maybe it starts with something much smaller: learning to see and appreciate the natural world around you.
