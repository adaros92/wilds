I spent my starting bonus and every long weekend and extended break during my senior year of college flying down to South America to visit my girlfriend. On paper it made no sense. The flights were expensive and flying from Pittsburgh to Georgetown, Guyana was not easy. I was supposed to be saving the money for a lease deposit and sensible start to adult life in NYC. But a long distance relationship from different continents has a way of making a bank balance feel like a very small thing.

So I went. Again and again. I wrote papers on the tray table at thirty thousand feet and studied for exams in the glow of a phone, jet lag and deadlines tangled together in a way that somehow felt like the most alive I'd ever been. There is a specific ache to loving someone across an ocean, but there was also the moment that made all of it worth it: walking out of the arrivals hall and finding her face in the crowd, the whole exhausting journey collapsing into the few seconds it took to reach her. 

She is now my wife so you could say I was making a completely different type of investment. But, what I didn't understand at the time was that those trips weren't really about the money or even about her. They were a valuable lesson about what it means to leave the version of the world you already know and let a different one rearrange you.

Trading my dorm in Pittsburgh for a rickety wooden house and a hammock in the tropics had a way of resizing my problems. The things I'd been stressing about back home got very small very fast when I was somewhere that ran on entirely different rules. Travel doesn't erase your worries, but it does show you how much of what you carry is just the weight of your own routine.

Mostly it made me realize how much I'd been handed. An education, the freedom to travel, opportunities I'd done nothing to earn, and how casually I was treating them. I was, after all, skipping some of those very classes to be there with her. Not my finest logic, but I don't regret it. Travel just made me a little more aware of what I had, and a little less inclined to waste the rest of it.

Every trip gave me something I didn't know I was missing, and every time I came home a slightly different person than the one who left. That, I think, is the real reason to travel. Not to escape your life, but to return to it with clearer eyes. The world is far larger than the small version of it we carry around in our heads, and you only find that out by leaving to look.

Now none of that was exactly new to me at that point in my life. I was born in Cuba and lived in Venezuela before coming to the US. Once my family got their footing in the US, they began traveling and taking me along with them. For people who had left everything behind to start over, seeing the world wasn't really a luxury. It was a kind of gratitude and way of enjoying the freedom they worked so hard to earn. I was fortunate to grow up understanding that home is something you carry and remake, and that the map is far bigger than the small piece of it you happen to be standing on.

But this time was different. It was the first time I was doing the traveling on my own, planning the trips and booking the flights. It felt like a celebration of my own independence, proof that the life my family had worked so hard for had actually taken root in me. 

It also reminded me of everything we had left behind. The traveling we did when I was growing up was to the safe, polished corners of the world. Nice Caribbean beaches and European cultural havens, never developing nations. My parents wanted nothing to do with that. They had spent close to forty years in communist Cuba, and they had earned the right to spend their time somewhere that asked nothing of them. Yet there I was, flying willingly into exactly the kind of place they had spent their whole lives trying to leave, and finding something there worth coming back for. Even if I was really just visiting a girl.

That girl grew up in a comfortable college town and earned her master's at Yale with the money her parents saved for her education. By every conventional measure she had a ticket to whatever high-earning career she wanted. Instead, she took that education deep into some of the most remote corners of Guyana to work on public health, in villages you can only reach by boat or a small plane.

I, meanwhile, had just signed an offer at a corporate bank in New York, chosen in no small part because it felt like it's what I had to do given the sacrifices made to get me to where I was. So watching her do this genuinely confused me. I couldn't understand why someone with her options would willingly choose the harder, poorer, less comfortable road. But the longer I sat with that confusion, the more it turned back on me. Why was I so certain my path was the sensible one? What exactly was I optimizing my life for, and who had decided that was the point?

That, more than any landscape, is what traveling at that point in life did to me. It set me down beside a life built on an entirely different set of values and made me ask whether mine held up. You don't get that from a vacation. You get it from going far enough off the familiar map, and staying long enough, that the way you've always lived stops looking like the only way.

For all the questions it stirred up, that stretch of my life was also just plain fun. Being based in Guyana turned it into a launchpad for places I never would have thought to prioritize. We spent a weekend on a remote beach you can only reach by boat, watching leatherback turtles haul themselves ashore to nest under the stars. We flew to French Guiana and pointed a rented Mini Cooper west toward Suriname, breaking up the drive with a catamaran out to Devil's Island, the notorious French penal colony that swallowed prisoners for a century between 1852 and 1952. We wandered the Dutch architecture of Paramaribo. We stood at the base of Kaieteur Falls, the largest single-drop waterfall in the world, with almost no one else around. We rented cars in Tobago and Jamaica and drove until the road ran out, stopping at one impossibly perfect beach after another.

None of these were on any list I'd made for my life. They happened because I was there, and being there made them easy. A place I'd never have flown to on its own was suddenly a weekend trip, and a bucket-list waterfall was just a short hop away. Basing yourself somewhere new doesn't only show you that one spot, it puts everything around it in reach.

More than anything, the traveling built us. Figuring out a new country together, getting lost, talking our way out of the occasional jam, turns out to be a quick way to learn who someone actually is. You find out how a person handles discomfort and uncertainty, the stuff a nice dinner date never surfaces. By the time we'd navigated a handful of countries side by side, we were pretty sure we could navigate a life.

That's held up. Those trips are still the memories we come back to most, and the habit we built, going to see things for ourselves instead of just reading about them, has made our life together a lot richer. Not in the bank-account sense I used to worry about, but in stories, in perspective, and in a shared sense that the world is worth going out into.

Today we travel very differently. We've traded the motels for nice hotels and the roadside diners for restaurants with tablecloths. Life is busier now, so we mostly use travel to rest and unplug rather than to prove anything to ourselves.

And that's fine. There's a time for roughing it and another for ordering room service. But I hope we never fully lose the old way of doing it, because the trips that shaped us weren't the comfortable ones. They were the ones where we got a little lost and a little uncomfortable. Those memories still live on. They're the reason a hard road has never scared us, and the reason that, even now, the fastest way back to who we are is to throw a few things in a bag and go see something new.
